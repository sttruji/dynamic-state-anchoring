\begin{abstract}
Latent chain-of-thought (CoT) models such as CODI and SIM-CoT reason by feeding continuous hidden states back into the model
for a fixed number of steps. We set out to make them robust to extra recurrent computation with Dynamic State Anchoring
(DSA), a gated, persistent anchor state that stores completed intermediate conclusions. On GPT-2, DSA's learned write
controller never learned when to write, and no write policy improved accuracy. Testing the proposal's premise directly, we
find that published CODI and SIM-CoT checkpoints lose accuracy when run past their 6 trained latent steps even when their
latent states are stable: appending exact copies of one latent cache entry reproduces SIM-CoT's whole loss, because repeated
keys draw the answer's attention away from the early latents. This reframes DSA as a gate over which latent cache entries
the answer can see. In a preregistered study of four checkpoints (CODI and SIM-CoT at GPT-2 and LLaMA-3.2-1B scale) on 3,818
held-out questions from GSM8K, GSM-Hard, MultiArith and SVAMP, the drift appears on all four. A position control splits it
exactly into two mechanisms: dilution by near-copy entries, which dominates at GPT-2 scale and which a training-free soft
deduplication removes, and the answer's growing rotary-position distance from the latents, which dominates at 1B and which
re-anchoring the answer removes. Together these hold accuracy at 24 steps within 2 points of the 6-step score on all four
models. No policy, trained gate or further fine-tuning turns the extra depth into accuracy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Continuous or latent chain-of-thought methods replace written reasoning with a short sequence of hidden states. Coconut
\citep{hao2025coconut} feeds the last hidden state back as the next input embedding; CODI \citep{shen2025codi} trains such a
loop by self-distillation from an explicit-CoT teacher; SIM-CoT \citep{wei2025simcot} adds step-level supervision of each
latent. The published CODI-style checkpoints all run a fixed budget of 6 latent steps. Looped and recurrent-depth
transformers suggest that extra iterations can buy extra computation \citep{saunshi2025reasoning, fan2025looped}, which
raises a natural question for latent CoT: does running more latent steps than the model was trained with help, hurt, or do
nothing?

Our project proposal asked this question with a specific hypothesis. Intermediate conclusions, we argued, become corrupted as
latent states pass through many recurrent transformations, so a model that selectively preserves completed conclusions in a
persistent \emph{anchor} state should stay accurate, or improve, when given more iterations. We called the method Dynamic
State Anchoring (DSA) and planned to compare a learned, hard-gated write controller with no-write, every-loop and periodic
write policies on GPT-2.

This report follows the project as it actually unfolded. The original DSA design failed: its controller never learned when
to write, and no write policy beat writing nothing (Section~\ref{sec:original-failure}). Rather than scale a mechanism with
no measured benefit, we tested the premise on the published checkpoints. They do lose accuracy past their trained depth,
but not mainly because their states deteriorate: SIM-CoT's latents are stable and keep their question content, yet it loses
as many answers as CODI, and appending exact copies of one cache entry reproduces its entire loss
(Section~\ref{sec:premise}). Every latent step appends a key to the cache, and repeated keys take attention away from the
early latents that carry the reasoning. That finding turned DSA from a gated memory into a gate over which latent cache
entries the answer can see (Section~\ref{sec:gate}). A learned version of that gate mostly rediscovered ``keep the first six
entries'', and a training-free soft deduplication did as well or better. We then froze a plan and tested the diagnosis on
untouched data and at a second scale (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}).

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A negative result for the original DSA: on GPT-2, a learned anchor-write controller trained with conclusion labels
    performs at chance, and anchoring adds no accuracy over no-write, every-loop or periodic controls.
  \item The first measurements, to our knowledge, of CODI and SIM-CoT run past their trained latent depth: on held-out data,
    all four checkpoints we test lose accuracy at 24 steps.
  \item A position control that splits that loss exactly into two mechanisms: dilution by near-copy cache entries, which
    dominates at GPT-2 scale, and rotary-position distance between the answer and the latents, which dominates at 1B.
  \item A matching training-free repair, soft deduplication plus, under RoPE, re-anchoring the answer, that holds accuracy at
    24 steps within 2 points of the 6-step score on all four models.
  \item Evidence that neither the repair, a trained latent gate, nor further fine-tuning turns extra depth into accuracy for
    these checkpoints.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background: latent CoT with appended latents}
\label{sec:background}

We study CODI-style models. The question's tokens are followed by a begin-of-thought token. At each latent step $t = 1,
\dots, T$, the model runs one forward step whose input embedding is $z_t = P(h_{t-1})$, a learned projection $P$ of the
previous step's last-layer hidden state. Each step therefore \emph{appends} one key/value entry per layer to the cache, and
nothing is overwritten. After $T$ steps an end-of-thought token is fed and the answer is decoded greedily, attending to the
question and to all $T$ latent entries. Every checkpoint we use was trained with $T = 6$.

\paragraph{Why repeated entries matter.} If a key appears $k$ times in a softmax, the group receives exactly the weight one
copy would receive with its logit raised by $\log k$. When a loop settles, later latents become near-copies of earlier ones,
and the readout's attention shifts toward them however stable the states are. Guarantees that a settled loop is safe to run
deeper assume the readout sees the current state in a fixed context \citep{viakhirev2026thinkshallow,
blayney2026mechanistic}; append-only latent CoT breaks that assumption.

\paragraph{Models.} CODI GPT-2 and SIM-CoT GPT-2 (124M parameters; \citealp{radford2019gpt2}) use learned absolute position
embeddings. CODI and SIM-CoT on LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} use rotary position embeddings (RoPE;
\citealp{su2024roformer}), whose attention depends on the distance between query and key positions.

\section{The original Dynamic State Anchoring}
\label{sec:original}

The proposal added to a shared GPT-2 backbone four latent workspace vectors, one persistent anchor vector, an anchor
projection head and a scalar write controller. At each loop $t$ the workspace $Z_t$ is updated from the question $X$, the
previous anchor $A_{t-1}$ and the previous workspace; a mean-pooled summary $u_t$ yields a candidate $C_t = W_a u_t + b_a$
and a write probability $p_t = \sigma(w_g^\top u_t + b_g)$; and a hard gate $g_t = \mathbb{1}[p_t \geq 0.5]$ decides whether
$A_t = (1 - g_t) A_{t-1} + g_t C_t$ replaces the stored anchor.

Write timing was supervised with a deterministic annotator that inserts an \texttt{<SA>} marker after each locally verified
arithmetic conclusion in a GSM8K-Aug rationale \citep{deng2023implicit}, excluding the final answer; it marks 613,547
conclusions on 83.5\% of training rows. Marked positions were mapped to loop indices, and the explicit teacher's hidden states
before each marker supplied anchor targets. Training combined answer cross-entropy, explicit-CoT cross-entropy, a binary
cross-entropy on the write probabilities and a normalized alignment loss on anchor candidates, weighted $1 : 1 : 0.1 : 0.1$.
For stable recurrence we adapted the Fully Looped Transformer (FLT; \citealp{fu2026flt}): previous-loop memory vectors supply
the queries at every block. Training started from the LOTUS GPT-2 CoT checkpoint \citep{fan2026lotus}, and the controls were
no writes (\pol{static}), \pol{every\_loop} and \pol{periodic} (every fourth loop), each sharing the learned arm's
initialization, data order and supervision.

\section{Why the original design failed}
\label{sec:original-failure}

\paragraph{Small pilots.} Two pilots trained on 32 questions for 200 updates and scored 128 validation questions. With the
standard recurrence at $K = 16$ loops, answer-gradient norms grew from about $3 \times 10^2$ at one loop to $2.4 \times
10^6$, every update hit the clipping threshold, and learned and static arms each scored 3/128. The FLT recurrence at $K = 8$
cut median gradient norms 88--279-fold and fit 29--31 of the 32 training questions, but validation stayed at 2--3/128. In
both pilots the controller's ranking of true conclusion loops was at chance (AUROC 0.510 and 0.507), and it wrote far more
often than the labels asked: on 58.6\% of loops against a labeled rate of 13.4\% in the $K = 16$ pilot.

\paragraph{The data-scale run.} With FLT fixed at $K = 8$, four write policies trained on 512 questions for 1,024 updates and
were scored on all 500 validation questions (Table~\ref{tab:datascale}). No writes scored highest, and no pairwise difference
reached $p < 0.05$. The controller's write rate fell from 91\% of loops early in training to zero; at inference it made 8
writes in 4,000 loop decisions, and its event AUROC was 0.513. Fine-tuning also damaged the shared explicit route: on 64
validation questions the original checkpoint answered 25 correctly and the fine-tuned routes 13--17.

\paragraph{A stronger recipe did not help either.} We then adopted the parts of CODI's GPT-2 recipe that DSA shares (LoRA rank
128, learning rate $3 \times 10^{-3}$, 128 records per update, answer-position distillation) and trained for one pass over
32,768 records, 256 updates or 0.2\% of CODI's training. The explicit route was preserved (205/500 on validation, against
212 for the original checkpoint and 206 for CODI), but latent answers reached only 37/500 with no writes. Every-loop and
periodic arms on identical batches scored 44 and 45 ($p = 0.34$ and $0.23$ against no writes). The loop had essentially
settled by 4 iterations: 4 and 8 loops gave the same answer on 477--484 of 500 questions. Note that \pol{every\_loop} overwrites the anchor with a projection of the current workspace, so only
the periodic and learned arms ever hold a value across loops.

\paragraph{Decision.} We parked the design. The controller had not learned event timing, validation accuracy was poor, extra
loops added nothing, and no write policy beat writing nothing. Scaling it to LLaMA, as the proposal planned, had no evidence
behind it. What remained untested was the premise itself.

\section{Testing the premise: drift past the trained depth}
\label{sec:premise}

We measured the published CODI GPT-2 and SIM-CoT GPT-2 checkpoints, without training, on the 500-question GSM8K-Aug
validation set (\texttt{gsm8k\_valid}), running the latent loop for more than 6 steps. All analyses in this section are
exploratory, and this set was reused for discovery throughout.

\paragraph{Both models drift.} CODI answers 206 questions correctly at 6 steps, 192 at 12 and 173 at 24 (24 against 6: 14
gained, 47 lost, $p < 0.001$); SIM-CoT answers 204, 185 and 170. Two preregistered ideas for exploiting or stopping the drift
failed: agreement between depths was a weaker confidence signal than the 6-step answer's own token log-probability (SVAMP
AUROC 0.81 against 0.91), and re-feeding the step-6 latent in place of new ones did not beat the plain loop (165 against 173
at 24 steps).

\paragraph{But not because the states deteriorate.} CODI's latents alternate between two states \citep[as in][]{li2026pmps},
and their question-specific content decays: a step-24 latent identifies its own question among the 500 only 67.4\% of the
time. SIM-CoT's latents instead settle (the cosine between neighbouring steps is about 0.95 from step 6 on) and keep their
content (97.6\% identification at step 24). Yet SIM-CoT loses as many answers as CODI.

\paragraph{Copies alone reproduce the loss.} After 6 genuine steps, we appended exact copies of the step-6 key/value entry
until the cache held 24 latent entries. On SIM-CoT this scores 172, against 170 for the real 24-step loop: copies reproduce
its entire loss. On CODI, single copies score 196 and copies of the last pair 188, against 173: about a third to a half of
its loss, with the rest coming from the changing content of its new entries.

\paragraph{The readout's attention moves.} The answer's first token puts 0.158 of its attention on latents 1--6 after 6 steps
in CODI and 0.033 after 24; in SIM-CoT the share falls from 0.099 to 0.036. Extra entries take attention from the early
latents, which matter most: removing entry 1 alone costs 0.42--0.54 nats of gold-answer log-probability, while removing any
later single entry costs almost nothing.

\section{DSA reframed: a gate over latent cache entries}
\label{sec:gate}

If the cache, not the state, is the problem, the thing to anchor is \emph{which latent entries the answer can see}. The
reframed DSA scores each latent entry when it is produced, adds $\log g_j$ to every attention logit pointing at entry $j$ (the
exact counterpart of the $\log k$ that copies add), and keeps at most 6 entries, the trained count, evicting the
lowest-scoring one when the budget is exceeded. A budget penalty $0.1 \cdot \max(0, \sum_j g_j - 6)$ drives the soft gate
during training. The mechanism itself is prior art for text caches
\citep{trimkv2026, leviathan2025selective, lin2025fox}; what was new to test was whether it rescues latent CoT past its
trained depth.

\paragraph{Soft deduplication.} A training-free alternative cancels the log-count boost directly. Once there are more than 6
latent entries, each entry $j$ receives the additive bias $-\log c_j$, where $c_j$ is the number of latent entries whose fed
latents have cosine similarity at least 0.95 with entry $j$'s. A cluster of $k$ exact copies then draws the attention of a
single entry. The bias applies to later latent steps as well as to the answer.

\paragraph{Stage 0: eviction at fixed weights.} With at most 6 latent entries visible at 24 steps, keeping the first six
entries restores the 6-step score on both models, and dropping near-duplicates nearly does (Table~\ref{tab:stage0}). Recency
and random eviction hurt, and so do rules that score entries by attention or by whether a logit-lens decode matches a gold
intermediate result. That decode label, which the reframed proposal meant to supervise the gate with, predicted an entry's
causal effect worse than chance (AUROC 0.41--0.45), so it was dropped.

\paragraph{Stage 1: fine-tuning with the gate.} The main hypothesis (H2) was that a model fine-tuned at 12 steps under the
gated budget beats the same model fine-tuned at 6 (\pol{ft6}) on problems with 4 or more rationale steps, at $p < 0.025$,

arms ran; CODI GPT-2 replicated the three most informative (Table~\ref{tab:stage1}). H2 failed on both models, so the
planned stop rule fired.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No 12-step arm used the extra steps.} On the 173 problems with 4+ steps, the SIM-CoT gates scored 26 and 28
    against \pol{ft6}'s 31, and the CODI gate 21 against 30.
  \item \textbf{The gate rediscovered ``keep the first six''.} On SIM-CoT it kept exactly entries 1--6 on 196 of 500
    questions and matched soft dedup (206 against 207 at 24 steps).
  \item \textbf{On CODI the gate failed.} It learned to keep one of CODI's two alternating phases, and with eviction it
    scored 168 at 24 steps.
  \item \textbf{Soft dedup held the 6-step score on both base models with no training}: 207 against 204 on SIM-CoT and 201
    against 206 on CODI.
\end{itemize}

\section{Held-out and scale evaluation}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Preregistration.} The plan, models, data, policies and decision rules below were written after Stage 1 and frozen,
with a recorded SHA-256 hash, before any run. One amendment, adding re-anchoring (Section~\ref{sec:anchoring}) and an
exploratory fine-tuning arm at 1B, was written after the main runs and before any run of what it added; it changed no
earlier rule.

\paragraph{Models.} Four published checkpoints, all trained with 6 latent steps: SIM-CoT GPT-2, CODI GPT-2, CODI on
LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct and SIM-CoT on LLaMA-3.2-1B. For the LLaMA models we merged the published LoRA adapters into the base
weights at FP32. Our port reproduces their published GSM8K accuracy: 55.5\% for CODI-1B (published 55.6\%) and 55.7\% for
SIM-CoT-1B (published 56.1\%). A 3B SIM-CoT model was optional and was not run.

\paragraph{Data.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Discovery:} \texttt{gsm8k\_valid} (500), already explored for the GPT-2 models.
  \item \textbf{Primary held-out pool (2,818):} GSM8K test (1,319; \citealp{cobbe2021gsm8k}), in distribution; GSM-Hard
    (1,319; \citealp{gao2023pal}) and MultiArith (180; \citealp{roy2015multiarith}), out of distribution. None had been
    scored past 6 steps or under any policy. GSM-Hard is GSM8K test with larger numbers, so the two are correlated.
  \item \textbf{Secondary:} SVAMP (1,000; \citealp{patel2021svamp}), reported separately because an earlier CODI study used it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Policies.} All decoding is greedy, with a budget of 6 latent entries where one applies.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \pol{plain}: every latent entry, at 6, 12 and 24 steps.
  \item \pol{keep\_first}: only entries 1--6 stay visible; later steps are still computed.
  \item \pol{dedup\_soft}: soft deduplication (Section~\ref{sec:gate}).
  \item \pol{logn}: every latent entry gets $-\log(n/6)$ once there are $n > 6$, a length-scaling rival.
  \item \pol{shift}: 6 latent steps, with the answer placed at the positions it would have after 24 steps. This
    position-only control is the new ingredient.
  \item \pol{gate}: the 6 highest-scoring entries, for gated checkpoints.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Decision rules.} Each is tested per model on the primary pool with exact two-sided paired sign tests and 95\%
paired Wald confidence intervals on accuracy differences. The noninferiority margin is 2 points, about the single-seed
noise seen in Stage 1.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{D1, drift:} \pol{plain}@24 is below \pol{plain}@6 ($p < 0.05$).
  \item \textbf{D2, the soft-dedup fix (primary):} \pol{dedup\_soft}@24 beats \pol{plain}@24 ($p < 0.05$), and the lower
    end of the CI on \pol{dedup\_soft}@24 $-$ \pol{plain}@6 is above $-2$ points.
  \item \textbf{D3, the budget fix:} the same for \pol{keep\_first}@24.
  \item \textbf{D4, position control (descriptive):} whether \pol{shift}@6 costs more than 2 points against \pol{plain}@6.
  \item \textbf{G, a free gain:} \pol{dedup\_soft} at 12 or 24 beats \pol{plain}@6 ($p < 0.025$ each).
\end{itemize}
The paper may claim that the fix generalizes only if D2 holds on every model where D1 holds, and that it holds across scale
only if a 1B model shows both D1 and D2.

\paragraph{Engineering checks.} A batched evaluator reproduced Stage 1's \texttt{gsm8k\_valid} counts exactly on both GPT-2
models. For LLaMA, latent biases enter as a 4D additive attention mask, and we checked that a zero bias reproduces the
model's own mask exactly and that $k$ copies each given $-\log k$ reproduce a single entry's logits within $10^{-4}$. All
runs used Colab GPUs (A100, and an RTX PRO 6000 for the last fine-tuning runs).

\subsection{The drift is real on every model}

D1 holds on all four checkpoints: running 24 latent steps instead of 6 costs 1.5 to 4.8 points on untouched data
(Figure~\ref{fig:depth}; Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:rules}). No policy at any depth significantly beats stopping at
6 steps on the primary pool (the closest is SIM-CoT GPT-2's \pol{plain}@12, 806 against 799, $p = 0.51$), so G fails
everywhere. The one exception in the whole study is a secondary-set result: on SVAMP, SIM-CoT GPT-2 with soft dedup at 24
steps scores 415 against 401 ($p = 0.016$), which we do not count as a claim given the number of comparisons.

\subsection{Soft dedup fixes GPT-2 but not 1B}

D2 holds on both GPT-2 models: soft dedup at 24 steps beats the plain loop and lands within half a point of the 6-step score.
The first-six budget (D3) holds there too. On both 1B models D2 fails: soft dedup barely improves on the plain loop and stays
1.7 and 2.8 points below the 6-step score. By the plan's rules, ``the fix generalizes'' and ``it holds across scale'' are
not supported.

\subsection{Position versus content}
\label{sec:split}

latents 1--6 and starts 18 positions later than usual), and they produced identical text on every question for all four

\emph{content} part, \pol{plain}@24 $-$ \pol{shift}@6, the effect of entries 7--24 at a fixed answer position
(Table~\ref{tab:split}).

\subsection{Re-anchoring the answer under RoPE}
\label{sec:anchoring}

Because RoPE makes attention depend only on relative position, the answer's distance from the latents can be repaired
exactly, without recomputing anything: rotating a cached key by a position offset is the same as having computed it at the
new position. The amendment added two policies for the LLaMA models. \pol{anchor} runs the plain loop and then, before the
readout, moves each later entry that is a near-copy of an earlier one to that copy's position and gives every other later
entry the next free position; the answer starts right after the last position used. \pol{dedup\_anchor} does the same after
a soft-dedup loop. A check on the real weights confirmed the rotation: moving every key and the query 18 positions changes
no logit by more than $10^{-3}$.

Its rules applied D2's criteria to \pol{dedup\_anchor}@24 (R1, primary) and to \pol{anchor}@24 (R2), and asked whether
either beats \pol{plain}@6 (G2). \textbf{R1 holds on both 1B models} (Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:rules}): at 24 steps,
\pol{dedup\_anchor} beats the plain loop by 1.0 and 2.6 points and stays within 0.8 and 0.9 points of the 6-step score,
recovering 28 of CODI-1B's 50 lost answers and 73 of SIM-CoT-1B's 99. R2 holds for CODI-1B, whose loss was all position, but
not for SIM-CoT-1B, which also has a content part and needs soft dedup as well, as the split predicts. G2 fails: the best
deeper result, \pol{dedup\_anchor} at 12 steps, is 0.4 points below 6 steps on both models, and the remaining gap at 24
steps, though inside the margin, is significant ($p \approx 0.005$). On SVAMP R1 also holds, with \pol{dedup\_anchor}@24
within 0.5 points of \pol{plain}@6. On \texttt{gsm8k\_valid}, re-anchoring needs only 1.4 (CODI-1B) and 4.0 (SIM-CoT-1B)
positions past entry 6 on average, but restores only part of the attention on latents 1--6 (Table~\ref{tab:attention}).

\subsection{Trained gates and further fine-tuning}

\paragraph{GPT-2.} We retrained the top-6 gate with a bug fixed (Stage 1 trained it with ties at the cut computed in bf16).
On SIM-CoT GPT-2 it holds depth (817 at 12 steps, 813 at 24 on the primary pool) but does not beat \pol{ft6} at 24 steps
(813 against 806, $p = 0.63$); it again keeps exactly entries 1--6, on 190 of 500 validation questions. On CODI GPT-2 it still fails (652 at
24 steps against 760 for \pol{ft6}), keeping one of CODI's two phases, so the bug did not cause Stage 1's failure.

\paragraph{1B.} As an exploratory addition, we fine-tuned SIM-CoT-1B with the Stage 1 recipe (full fine-tune, one pass over
the same 32,768 problems) as \pol{ft6}, \pol{ft12} and the fixed gate (Table~\ref{tab:partc}). No arm passed the rule, so
nothing was run on held-out data. More importantly, the fine-tune itself cost accuracy: \pol{ft6} scores 246 against the base
model's 277 at its own trained depth ($-6.2$ points, $p = 2 \times 10^{-4}$), and every arm ends below the base model. Its
answer-token cross-entropy is already 0.04--0.11 at the first update and stays roughly flat, against 0.24--0.37 falling by a
third to a half for the GPT-2 gates, which suggests SIM-CoT-1B already fits these training answers and the recipe has
little left to teach. Training at 12 steps does remove the drift (\pol{ft12}: 240 at 12 steps, 237 at 24), but from a lower
start.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why the original DSA failed.} The proposal assumed that latent states deteriorate over extra iterations and that
a gated memory could hold what they lose. Its controller never found a learnable signal in the conclusion labels, and at the
budgets we could afford the latent route stayed far below the explicit one. More fundamentally, the premise was wrong in an
instructive way: the published latent CoT models do lose accuracy with extra steps, but for SIM-CoT the states are not the
problem, and for all of them the cache and the answer's position are.

\paragraph{Append-only reasoning is not depth-safe.} Dynamical accounts of looped models predict that a loop which settles
can be run deeper safely \citep{viakhirev2026thinkshallow, blayney2026mechanistic}. Those guarantees assume a fixed
context. In append-only latent CoT, a settled state appended repeatedly still degrades the readout, and SIM-CoT is a clean
counterexample.

\paragraph{Repair, not gain.} Each mechanism has a matching training-free repair, and together they hold accuracy past the
trained depth on all four models. None of them, and neither a trained gate nor more fine-tuning, makes the extra steps
useful. This matches the broader pattern that methods stabilizing loops past their trained depth preserve peak accuracy
without exceeding it \citep{viakhirev2026thinkshallow}. Making extra latent steps add accuracy likely needs training that
targets depth directly, such as randomized loop counts \citep{kuo2026stabilizing} or architectures trained with more latents
\citep{wu2025pccot}, and, at 1B, a recipe that does not erode the base model.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Latent chain-of-thought.} Implicit CoT distills written reasoning into hidden states \citep{deng2023implicit}.
Coconut feeds the last hidden state back as the next input embedding \citep{hao2025coconut}; CODI trains such a loop by
self-distillation from an explicit-CoT teacher at the answer position \citep{shen2025codi}; SIM-CoT supervises each latent
step through an auxiliary decoder \citep{wei2025simcot}; and PMPS supervises latents with soft prototypes
\citep{li2026pmps}. PCCoT instead updates a fixed set of latents in parallel by Jacobi iteration, so its cache does not grow
\citep{wu2025pccot}, and pause tokens add unstructured extra computation \citep{goyal2024pause}. Our original design built on
LOTUS, which supervises parallel latent blocks inside a loop \citep{fan2026lotus}, and on FLT's stabilized recurrence
\citep{fu2026flt}. Several of these papers report that accuracy falls as the number of \emph{training} latents grows. CODI
peaks at 6 and attributes the decline to optimization, PMPS finds that CODI drops sharply at 8, and SIM-CoT traces Coconut's
collapse at larger counts to homogeneous latents. These are effects of the count a model is trained with. We found no work
that runs a trained checkpoint past its latent count at inference; the closest is LOTUS, which reports one extra iteration
(70.0\% at its trained depth, 69.3\% one step beyond).

\paragraph{Looped models and test-time depth.} Looped and recurrent-depth transformers reuse weights across iterations to
add computation \citep{saunshi2025reasoning, fan2025looped, geiping2025huginn, zhu2025ouro}. Running a recurrent network past
its training regime can degrade it, which \citet{bansal2022overthinking} called overthinking and fixed with input recall and
a progressive loss. Recent work explains depth degradation through the loop's dynamics.
\citet{viakhirev2026thinkshallow} sort recurrent operators into settling, marginal and drifting regimes and show that settling
operators do not degrade with added depth; \citet{blayney2026mechanistic} find that looped language models reach fixed
points and bound the change in attention by the change in the state; \citet{kuo2026stabilizing} stabilize extrapolation by
randomizing the number of loops during training. Bounding access to past loop states also rescues depth
\citep{li2026infiloop, recurtrace2026}, and Huginn and Ouro decode with a bounded or last-step cache. These models replace
their state at each iteration, so the readout sees the current state in a fixed context. Append-only latent CoT breaks that
assumption: each step adds a key, so a settled state appended repeatedly still draws attention, and SIM-CoT is a
counterexample to the view that settling implies depth safety. The two families also disagree on what a bounded cache should
keep. Ouro loses almost nothing by keeping only its last step, whereas in CODI-style models a recency window hurts and the
early entries must be kept (Table~\ref{tab:stage0}). Finally, the methods that stabilize loops past their trained depth
restore accuracy but do not exceed the peak \citep{viakhirev2026thinkshallow}, as our repairs do.

\paragraph{Attention dilution, length and position.} When logits are bounded, softmax attention must disperse as keys are
added \citep{velickovic2025softmax}; multiplying logits by $\log n$ \citep{chiang2022overcoming} or scaling them with length
\citep{nakanishi2025ssmax} counteracts this, and attention sinks absorb surplus mass \citep{xiao2024streamingllm}. Repeating
a token makes final representations arbitrarily close as the sequence grows \citep{barbero2024glasses}. Our log-count identity
is the same algebra applied to duplicated latent keys. A length-based correction such as our \pol{logn} policy rescales every
latent entry alike, so it cannot separate near-copies from the entries they copy, whereas soft dedup can. On the position
side, RoPE makes attention depend on relative distance \citep{su2024roformer}, which is what lets a rotation re-anchor
cached keys exactly.

\paragraph{KV-cache eviction and learned gates.} Fixed rules for evicting text-cache entries keep heavy hitters by
cumulative attention \citep{zhang2023h2o}, keep sinks and a recent window \citep{xiao2024streamingllm}, or remove redundant
entries by key similarity \citep{cai2025rkv, park2025keydiff}. Learned variants add a data-dependent bias to the attention
logits: Selective Attention \citep{leviathan2025selective}, the Forgetting Transformer \citep{lin2025fox}, Dynamic Context
Pruning \citep{anagnostidis2023dcp} and Dynamic Memory Sparsification \citep{lancucki2025dms}, the last two training soft and
pruning hard. TRIM-KV \citep{trimkv2026} learns a retention score with a capacity penalty and evicts the lowest-scoring entry,
the mechanism closest to our gate. In latent reasoning, KaVa compresses a teacher's cache by redundancy-aware eviction to
build distillation targets for a CODI-family student \citep{kuzina2025kava}, and AGCLR adds learned gates over a separate
memory vector in a Coconut loop \citep{farhan2026agclr}. Neither gates the latent cache at inference or runs past the
trained depth. Soft dedup differs from similarity-based eviction in three ways: it reweights instead of evicting, it groups
entries by their fed latent vectors rather than by keys, and it exactly cancels the log-count boost of copies. We found no
prior test of it.

\paragraph{Supervising intermediate latent steps.} The original DSA, like SIM-CoT and PMPS, supervised latent computation
with gold intermediate results. CODI reports that its latents often decode to the reference intermediate values, but PMPS
finds that logit-lens readouts do not map one-to-one onto CoT steps, which is consistent with our decode label predicting an
entry's causal effect worse than chance. Process reward models for latent reasoning remain weak
\citep{wang2025inference}.

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to make latent chain-of-thought robust to extra recurrent computation with a gated anchor state. That design
failed, but testing its premise showed why such models lose accuracy past their trained depth: appended near-copy latents
dilute the answer's attention, and under RoPE the answer drifts away from the latents it needs. A training-free repair matched
to each mechanism holds accuracy at 24 steps within 2 points of the 6-step score on four checkpoints at two scales. Turning
the extra depth into accuracy remains open.

\section*{Limitations}

Every training arm used one seed; Stage 1 suggests single-seed noise of about 10 answers in 500. The GPT-2 analyses in
Sections~\ref{sec:premise} and~\ref{sec:gate} reused one validation set for discovery. Re-anchoring was designed after the
main held-out runs had revealed the position cost on the same held-out sets; its rules were fixed before it ran, but it is
not a clean confirmation, which needs a set not yet used. GSM-Hard is derived from GSM8K test, so the primary pool is not
three independent sets. Our SIM-CoT GPT-2 port scores 39.3\% on GSM8K test against 42.6\% reported by its authors (CODI
GPT-2: 42.5\% against 43.7\%). We used greedy decoding and arithmetic word problems only, and did not run the 3B model. The
1B fine-tuning arms were scored on validation only.
